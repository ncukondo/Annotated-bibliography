# Grounded theory

## What is grounded theory?

Grounded theory is a systematic methodolgy consisting of several flexible strategies for constructing theory through analyzing qualitative data [@Charmaz2017-rx]

## Where to use?

Grounded theory's emphasis on analyzing processes makes it well-suited for investigating the complex social and experiential dimensions of medical education [@Watling2012-vz]

## Methodology

The method involves simultaneous data collection and analysis, with strategies for refining the emerging categories [@Watling2012-vz]

## Constructivist grounded theory

Constructivist grounded theory distinguishes itself from its predecessors by shifting away from the positivist view that a researcher can remain objective and "discover" theory in the data. Instead, constructivist grounded theory acknowledges the active role that researchers play in constructing knowledge through their interactions with participants and data. [@Charmaz2017-rx]

### Methodology

#### Simultaneous Data Collection and Analysis

Like other forms of grounded theory, constructivist grounded theory adopts an iterative approach where data collection and analysis happen concurrently. As you gather data, you begin analyzing it, allowing early insights to shape subsequent data collection. This iterative process distinguishes grounded theory from many other qualitative research methods. For example, in their "influential experiences study" on physicians' learning, Watling and Lingard began analyzing interview transcripts as they were completed. This early analysis allowed them to identify unanticipated findings and areas requiring further exploration, which then guided subsequent interviews.

#### Coding

Coding involves organizing data into categories relevant to the emerging theory. Grounded theory emphasizes coding for actions and meanings, often using gerunds (verb forms ending in "-ing") to capture processes. Codes are not predetermined but emerge through your interactions with the data. Constructivist grounded theory recognizes that your interpretations and perspectives influence how you code data.

##### Initial Coding

You start with "close coding," focusing on smaller units of data like words, phrases, or sentences, to remain open to different theoretical possibilities. In their example on coding for topics and themes vs. using gerunds, Charmaz shows how initial coding with gerunds like "receiving friends' help" and "requesting regimen re-evaluation" can reveal processes that might otherwise be overlooked.

##### Focused Coding

As your analysis progresses, you develop broader categories that encompass related concepts, shifting from describing what the data is to conceptualizing what it means. Watling and Lingard provide an example of how they moved from initial codes like "learner attitude" and "independence/autonomy" to the broader categories of "learning conditions" and "learning cues."

#### Constant Comparison

This ongoing process of comparing data to data, data to codes, and codes to codes is central to grounded theory. It helps you define the characteristics and boundaries of categories and reveal relationships between them. Comparing outliers and "negative cases" – those that don't fit neatly into emerging categories – is particularly valuable. These outliers force you to refine categories and develop concepts that account for a wider range of data.

##### Memo Writing

Memo writing is crucial for capturing your evolving analytic thinking throughout the research process. Memos are informal reflections on codes, categories, and emerging insights. They help you move from describing the data to conceptualizing and theorizing about it. Watling and Lingard provide an example of a memo on "Determining Credibility," where they explore the factors that influence learners' judgments about the trustworthiness of feedback. This memo reflects how memo-writing facilitates moving from codes to a deeper understanding of the processes at play.

#####  Theoretical Sampling

Unlike sampling in quantitative research, which aims for representativeness, theoretical sampling is driven by your emerging theoretical analysis. Once you identify a key category, you purposefully sample data – through interviews, observations, or documents – to develop and refine that category. You continue this process until the category is "saturated" – that is, until no new properties or dimensions emerge from the data. Charmaz challenges the common misunderstanding of saturation as simply encountering repetitive data. Instead, she emphasizes that saturation in grounded theory means reaching a point where your theoretical categories are well-developed and further data collection is unlikely to yield new theoretical insights.
